Modeling and Analysis of Mechanical Systems — Derivatives, Gradients, Hessians and the Material Derivative

Created on November 15, 2026

2026   ·   Modeling and Analysis of Mechanical Systems

A derivative tells us how fast a function changes. With one input there is only one direction to move; with several inputs the rate depends on which way we move. This post goes from the ordinary derivative to the gradient and the directional derivative, then uses a ski hill to find the steepest way down. The second half builds the foundations of optimization: the Jacobian, Taylor series, gradient descent, and the Hessian for telling minima, maxima and saddle points apart, with a load-capacity design example. It ends with the material derivative — the rate of change seen by a moving fluid particle.

1. Derivative vs. gradient

Both measure the rate of change (slope) of a function, but for different kinds of functions:

  Derivative Gradient
Function single variable, $y=\phi(x)$ multivariable scalar field, $f(x,y,z)$
Definition $y’=\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\phi’(x)$ $\nabla f=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\hat i+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\hat j+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,\hat k$
Result a scalar a vector
Meaning slope of the curve at $x$ direction of steepest increase; its magnitude is that steepest slope

The derivative is the limit of the slope of a secant line,

\[\phi'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\phi(x+h)-\phi(x)}{h}.\]

A partial derivative applies the same idea along one coordinate, holding the others fixed: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h,y,z)-f(x,y,z)}{h}$. The gradient collects all of them into a column vector:

\[\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x\\ \partial f/\partial y\\ \partial f/\partial z\end{bmatrix}.\]

The gradient is defined for a scalar function of several variables. For a vector-valued function (several outputs) the analogue is the Jacobian matrix, whose rows are the gradients of each output.

A single partial derivative only gives the slope along a coordinate axis. To get the slope in an arbitrary direction we need the directional derivative.

2. Directional derivative

The directional derivative $D_{\hat u}f$ is the rate of change of $f$ when moving in the direction of a unit vector $\hat u$ ($\lvert\hat u\rvert=1$). It is a scalar: the projection of the gradient onto $\hat u$,

\[D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u=\lvert\nabla f\rvert\,\lvert\hat u\rvert\cos\theta=\lvert\nabla f\rvert\cos\theta,\]

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\hat u$. Since $-1\le\cos\theta\le1$, every directional derivative lies between $-\lvert\nabla f\rvert$ and $+\lvert\nabla f\rvert$:

Angle $\theta$ $D_{\hat u}f$ Meaning
$0$ $+\lvert\nabla f\rvert$ (maximum) $\hat u$ along $\nabla f$: steepest ascent, straight up the slope
$90^\circ$ $0$ $\hat u\perp\nabla f$: walking along a contour (level curve), no change in height
$180^\circ=\pi$ $-\lvert\nabla f\rvert$ (minimum) $\hat u$ against $\nabla f$: steepest descent, straight down the slope

So the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, $-\nabla f$ in the direction of steepest descent, and the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour lines of $f$. This is why the arrows on a contour map that show the fall line cross the contours at right angles.

Pitfall: normalize the direction first. The formula assumes $\lvert\hat u\rvert=1$. For a direction given as $\vec v=\hat i+\hat j$, use $\hat u=\vec v/\lvert\vec v\rvert=(\hat i+\hat j)/\sqrt2$. Using $\vec v$ directly multiplies the answer by $\lvert\vec v\rvert=\sqrt2$.

3. Ski-hill example

The topography of a ski hill is modeled by

\[f(x,y)=1200-0.05x^2-0.01y^2,\]

where $f$ is the elevation in metres at horizontal position $(x,y)$, also in metres. The summit is at the origin, $f(0,0)=1200$ m. You stand at $(10,50)$, where $f(10,50)=1200-5-25=1170$ m.

(a) The direction of steepest ascent, as a unit vector.

\[\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}-2(0.05)x\\-2(0.01)y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.1x\\-0.02y\end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla f(10,50)=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=-\hat i-\hat j .\]

$\lvert\nabla f\rvert=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2$ is the magnitude of the maximum slope. The steepest-ascent direction is

\[\hat u=\frac{\nabla f}{\lvert\nabla f\rvert}=\frac{-\hat i-\hat j}{\sqrt2}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\hat i+\hat j).\]

(b) The slope of steepest descent. Steepest descent points opposite to the gradient, along $\hat s=-\hat u=(\hat i+\hat j)/\sqrt2$, i.e. $\theta=180^\circ$:

\[D_{\hat s}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat s=\lvert\nabla f\rvert\,\lvert\hat s\rvert\cos180^\circ=-\lvert\nabla f\rvert=-\sqrt2\approx-1.41 .\]

The slope of steepest descent is the minimum directional derivative. Its units are metres of elevation per metre of horizontal travel: you drop about 1.41 m for every metre you move, a slope angle of $\arctan\sqrt2\approx54.7^\circ$ — a black-diamond run.

Checking other directions at $(10,50)$, with $\nabla f=(-1,-1)$:

Direction $\hat u$ $D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u$ Interpretation
east, $(1,0)$ $-1$ going downhill, but not the steepest way
along the contour, $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ $0$ traversing at constant height
straight at the summit, $(-0.196,-0.981)$ $+1.18$ uphill, but less steep than $\sqrt2$
steepest descent, $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ $-\sqrt2$ the fall line

Note the third row: the steepest way up does not point at the summit. The direction to the top, $(-10,-50)$, is $33.7^\circ$ away from $\nabla f$, because the hill is elliptical (it falls off 5 times faster in $x$ than in $y$). Following the gradient everywhere traces a curved path, $dy/dx=(0.02y)/(0.1x)\Rightarrow y=Cx^{0.2}$, here $y\approx31.5\,x^{0.2}$, which bends toward the $y$-axis before reaching the summit. Locally steepest is not globally shortest.

4. Gradient of a vector function: the Jacobian

For a scalar function $f(x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots)$ of many variables the gradient is the column vector of first partial derivatives,

\[\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x_1\\ \partial f/\partial x_2\\ \partial f/\partial x_3\\ \vdots\end{bmatrix}.\]

Near a point $\vec x_0$ it defines the best linear approximation of $f$: $f(\vec x)\approx f(\vec x_0)+\nabla f(\vec x_0)\cdot(\vec x-\vec x_0)$ (the tangent plane).

For a vector-valued function each component has its own gradient, and stacking them as rows gives the Jacobian matrix $J_{ij}=\partial v_i/\partial x_j$. Example:

\[\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}3x^2y\\3y^3\\7xz\end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad J=\nabla\vec v= \begin{bmatrix} \partial v_1/\partial x&\partial v_1/\partial y&\partial v_1/\partial z\\ \partial v_2/\partial x&\partial v_2/\partial y&\partial v_2/\partial z\\ \partial v_3/\partial x&\partial v_3/\partial y&\partial v_3/\partial z \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}6xy&3x^2&0\\0&9y^2&0\\7z&0&7x\end{bmatrix}.\]

Row $i$ is $(\nabla v_i)^T$. (Some texts, notably in continuum mechanics, define $\nabla\vec v$ as the transpose, so check the convention.) The Jacobian is to a vector function what the derivative is to a scalar one: $\vec v(\vec x)\approx\vec v(\vec x_0)+J(\vec x-\vec x_0)$.

5. Taylor series: the best linear and quadratic approximations

In one variable, around $x_0$:

\[f(x)\approx\underbrace{f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)}_{\text{best linear}}+\frac{f''(x_0)}{2!}(x-x_0)^2 ,\]

where adding the last term gives the best quadratic approximation. For $n$ variables, with $\vec x_0=(x_1^0,\dots,x_n^0)$:

\[f(\vec x)\approx f(\vec x_0)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x_i-x_i^0)+\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(x_i-x_i^0)(x_j-x_j^0),\]

with all derivatives evaluated at $\vec x_0$. In matrix form, with $\Delta\vec x=\vec x-\vec x_0$,

\[f(\vec x)\approx f(\vec x_0)+\nabla f^T\Delta\vec x+\tfrac12\,\Delta\vec x^T H\,\Delta\vec x, \qquad H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}.\]

$H$ is the Hessian matrix. For smooth functions the mixed partials are equal ($\partial^2f/\partial x\partial y=\partial^2f/\partial y\partial x$), so $H$ is symmetric. Note the factor $\tfrac12$, matching the $1/2!$ of the 1-D series.

6. Finding a minimum or maximum

Analytical route. At an interior minimum or maximum the slope is zero in every direction, so solve

\[\nabla f=\vec 0 .\]

This condition is necessary but not sufficient: the points that satisfy it are critical (stationary) points, which may be minima, maxima or saddle points (§8). For realistic engineering models — thousands of variables, $f$ given by a simulation rather than a formula — this system usually cannot be solved by hand. So we search numerically, using $\nabla f$ (and sometimes $H$) only at the points we visit. The condition $\nabla f=\vec0$ still matters: it is how we recognize that we have arrived.

7. Gradient descent

To minimize $f$, start at $\vec x_0$ and repeatedly step in a downhill direction $\vec p$:

\[\vec x_{k+1}=\vec x_k+\alpha\,\vec p_k .\]

Two things must be chosen at every step:

  1. The search direction $\vec p$. The only requirement is that $f$ decreases along it. By the best linear approximation, $f(\vec x_k+\alpha\vec p)\approx f(\vec x_k)+\alpha\,\nabla f\cdot\vec p$, so $\vec p$ is a descent direction whenever $\nabla f\cdot\vec p<0$ (angle with the gradient above $90^\circ$). The steepest choice is $\vec p=-\nabla f$ (§2), giving \(\vec x_{k+1}=\vec x_k-\alpha\,\nabla f(\vec x_k).\)
  2. The step size $\alpha>0$. Too small is slow; too large overshoots and can diverge, because the linear approximation only holds near $\vec x_k$. In practice $\alpha$ is either fixed (the “learning rate” in machine learning) or chosen by a line search that shrinks it until $f$ decreases enough.

To find a maximum, either step uphill, $\vec x_{k+1}=\vec x_k+\alpha\nabla f$ (gradient ascent), or minimize $-f$.

8. The Hessian: is it a cup, a dome or a saddle?

At a critical point $\nabla f=\vec0$, so the Taylor series reduces to $f(\vec x)-f(\vec x_0)\approx\tfrac12\Delta\vec x^TH\Delta\vec x$. The local shape is set by the quadratic form of $H$ — the same classification as for positive definite matrices in the matrix decomposition post. Since $H$ is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real:

all $\lambda>0$ positive definite cup ∪ local minimum
all $\lambda<0$ negative definite dome ∩ local maximum
mixed signs indefinite saddle saddle point (neither)
some $\lambda=0$, rest one sign semi-definite flat direction test is inconclusive

For $2\times2$ there is a shortcut, since $\det H=\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr}H=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$: $\det H<0$ means a saddle; $\det H>0$ means a minimum if $\operatorname{tr}H>0$ (equivalently $f_{xx}>0$) and a maximum if $\operatorname{tr}H<0$.

\[f(x,y)=12xy-x^3-y^3 .\]

Gradient and Hessian:

\[\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}12y-3x^2\\12x-3y^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^2f/\partial x^2&\partial^2f/\partial x\partial y\\\partial^2f/\partial y\partial x&\partial^2f/\partial y^2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-6x&12\\12&-6y\end{bmatrix}.\]

Critical points: $\nabla f=\vec0$ gives $y=x^2/4$ and $x=y^2/4$, so $x=x^4/64\Rightarrow x(x^3-64)=0$, i.e. $(0,0)$ and $(4,4)$.

At $(4,4)$: $\nabla f=(48-48,\ 48-48)=(0,0)$ ✓ and

\[H=\begin{bmatrix}-24&12\\12&-24\end{bmatrix},\qquad (-24-\lambda)^2-12^2=0\Rightarrow(-24-\lambda-12)(-24-\lambda+12)=0\Rightarrow\lambda=-36,\ -12 .\]

Both eigenvalues are negative: $H$ is negative definite, the surface is dome-shaped, and $(4,4)$ is a local maximum with $f(4,4)=192-64-64=64$. (Check: $\det H=432>0$, $\operatorname{tr}H=-48<0$.)

At $(0,0)$: $H=\begin{bmatrix}0&12\12&0\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda=\pm12$ — mixed signs, so a saddle point. Along $y=x$ the function first rises, along $y=-x$ it falls ($f=-12x^2$).

Local is not global. $(4,4)$ is the highest point nearby, but $f$ is unbounded: $f(-10,0)=1000>64$, and $f\to+\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. The Hessian test is purely local.

9. Why the test works: the 2-D second-derivative test

For two variables the Hessian is

\[H=\begin{bmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{bmatrix},\qquad f_{xy}=f_{yx}\ \text{(symmetric)},\qquad D=\det H=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2 .\]

The gradient is the slope; the Hessian is the curvature (bendiness) — how the slope itself changes as we move in different directions.

Step 1: Taylor expand around the critical point. Let $(a,b)$ be a critical point, so $f_x(a,b)=f_y(a,b)=0$, and take a small step $(h,k)$:

\[f(a+h,b+k)=f(a,b)+\underbrace{h f_x+k f_y}_{=0}+\tfrac12\left(f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2\right)+\dots\]

With all derivatives evaluated at $(a,b)$, the change is

\[\Delta f=f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)\approx\tfrac12\left(f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2\right).\]

$(a,b)$ is a minimum if $\Delta f>0$ for every small step, a maximum if $\Delta f<0$ for every step, and a saddle if the sign depends on the direction.

Step 2: complete the square. Assume $f_{xx}\ne0$ and multiply and divide by $f_{xx}$:

\[\Delta f\approx\frac{1}{2f_{xx}}\left[f_{xx}^2h^2+2f_{xx}f_{xy}hk+f_{xx}f_{yy}k^2\right] =\frac{1}{2f_{xx}}\Big[\underbrace{(f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k)^2}_{\ge0}+\underbrace{(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2)}_{D}\,k^2\Big].\]

(Expand $(f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k)^2=f_{xx}^2h^2+2f_{xx}f_{xy}hk+f_{xy}^2k^2$; the leftover is exactly $D\,k^2$.) The squared term is never negative, and is zero only on the line $f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k=0$. So the sign of $\Delta f$ is controlled by $f_{xx}$ and $D$.

Step 3: read off the cases.

Condition at $(a,b)$ Bracket $\Delta f$ Point
$D>0$, $f_{xx}>0$ sum of two non-negative terms, not both zero $>0$ in every direction local minimum (cup)
$D>0$, $f_{xx}<0$ positive, divided by negative $2f_{xx}$ $<0$ in every direction local maximum (peak)
$D<0$ $+(\cdot)^2$ and $-\lvert D\rvert k^2$ compete changes sign with direction saddle point
$D=0$ single perfect square $=0$ along one line inconclusive

Why the bracket can’t vanish when $D>0$: both terms are zero only if $k=0$ and then $f_{xx}h=0$, i.e. $h=0$ — no step at all.

Saddle ($D<0$), two test paths:

  • Move along $k=0$: the second term vanishes, $\Delta f=\tfrac12f_{xx}h^2$, which has the sign of $f_{xx}$.
  • Move along $f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k=0$: the first term vanishes, $\Delta f=Dk^2/(2f_{xx})$, which has the opposite sign of $f_{xx}$.

Since $\Delta f$ changes sign depending on the path, the point is neither a maximum nor a minimum.

Inconclusive ($D=0$): $\Delta f\approx(f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k)^2/(2f_{xx})$ is a single perfect square, which is zero along the line $f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k=0$. Along that line the second-order change vanishes, so third- or fourth-order terms decide. All of these have $D=0$ at the origin but behave differently:

$f(x,y)$ Origin is
$x^4+y^4$ minimum
$-(x^4+y^4)$ maximum
$x^4-y^4$ saddle
$x^2+y^3$ neither (saddle-like: $f<0$ for small $y<0$)

Why test $f_{xx}$ and not $f_{yy}$? Either works. At an extremum $D>0$, so $f_{xx}f_{yy}>f_{xy}^2\ge0$, which forces $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ to have the same sign. Checking either one gives the same conclusion. (If $f_{xx}=0$ then $D=-f_{xy}^2\le0$, so the point is a saddle when $f_{xy}\ne0$; otherwise complete the square in $k$ using $f_{yy}$ instead.)

Link to eigenvalues and LU. Completing the square is exactly Gaussian elimination (§4 of the decomposition post) applied to $H$: the pivots are $f_{xx}$ and $D/f_{xx}$, since

\[\Delta f\approx\tfrac12\left[f_{xx}\Big(h+\tfrac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}k\Big)^2+\frac{D}{f_{xx}}k^2\right].\]

For a symmetric matrix, the number of positive (and negative) pivots equals the number of positive (and negative) eigenvalues (Sylvester’s law of inertia). So “$f_{xx}>0$ and $D>0$” is the same statement as “both eigenvalues positive” (§8), checked without solving the characteristic equation. It is the $2\times2$ case of Sylvester’s criterion: a symmetric matrix is positive definite if all its leading principal minors ($f_{xx}$, then $\det H$) are positive.

10. Engineering example: maximum load-bearing capacity

The maximum load-bearing capacity $L$ (kN) of a machine foundation depends on two geometric features $x$ and $y$ (cm). Balancing weight against material strength gives the empirical expression

\[L(x,y)=12xy-x^3-y^3 .\]

Find the dimensions that maximize the load capacity.

Step 1 — Critical point.

\[\nabla L=\begin{bmatrix}12y-3x^2\\12x-3y^2\end{bmatrix}=\vec0 \;\Rightarrow\;4y=x^2,\quad 4x=y^2 .\]

Substituting $y=x^2/4$ into the second: $4x=x^4/16\Rightarrow x(x^3-64)=0$, so $x=0$ or $x=4$. The critical points are $(0,0)$ and $(4,4)$ cm. $(0,0)$ is a saddle (§8) and physically meaningless (no foundation), so the candidate is $(x,y)=(4,4)$ cm.

Step 2 — Second-derivative test.

\[H=\begin{bmatrix}-6x&12\\12&-6y\end{bmatrix}\;\xrightarrow{(4,4)}\;\begin{bmatrix}-24&12\\12&-24\end{bmatrix},\qquad D=(-24)(-24)-12^2=24^2-12^2=(24+12)(24-12)=432>0 .\]

$D>0$, so $(4,4)$ is a local minimum or maximum. $L_{xx}=-24<0$, so it is a maximum:

\[L_\text{max}=L(4,4)=12(16)-64-64=64\ \text{kN}.\]

(The eigenvalues $-36$ and $-12$ of §8 say the same thing.)

Is it the global maximum? In §8 we noted that on the whole plane it is not ($L(-10,0)=1000$). But dimensions must be non-negative, so only $x,y\ge0$ matters. There, $(4,4)$ is the only interior critical point; on the boundary $L=-x^3$ or $-y^3\le0$; and $L\to-\infty$ for large $x$ or $y$. So within the physical domain, 64 kN at $4\times4$ cm is the global maximum. Always check the domain and its boundary, not just $\nabla f=\vec0$.

Units: in an empirical fit like this the coefficients carry units (here 12 has units of kN/cm², and the cubic terms have an implicit 1 kN/cm³), which is why cm² and cm³ terms can be added to give kN.

11. Newton’s method: using the Hessian

Gradient descent uses only the linear (first-order) model. Newton’s method minimizes the quadratic model of §5 instead: setting the gradient of $f(\vec x_k)+\nabla f^T\Delta\vec x+\tfrac12\Delta\vec x^TH\Delta\vec x$ to zero gives

\[H(\vec x_k)\,\Delta\vec x=-\nabla f(\vec x_k),\qquad \vec x_{k+1}=\vec x_k+\Delta\vec x .\]

It picks the direction and the step size together, and near the optimum the number of correct digits roughly doubles each iteration. On the example above, from $(3,5)$ it reaches $(4,4)$ to six digits in 3 iterations, against about 50 for gradient ascent. The costs: forming and solving with the $n\times n$ Hessian (expensive for large $n$), and it heads for any critical point — including saddles — unless $H$ has the right definiteness. Quasi-Newton methods (e.g. BFGS) build an approximate Hessian from successive gradients, and are the usual compromise in engineering optimization.

12. The material derivative

So far $f$ depended on fixed coordinates. In a moving fluid we often care how a property $\phi$ (temperature, density, velocity) changes for a fluid particle as it travels. This rate is the material derivative, also called the substantial or total derivative, $D\phi/Dt$.

Two viewpoints.

  • Eulerian: stand on a bridge and watch the river through a fixed window $(x,y,z)$. The fields are $\phi(x,y,z,t)$, and $\partial\phi/\partial t$ is the local rate of change at that fixed point.
  • Lagrangian: ride a boat (a fluid particle) and record what you experience. The rate of change you feel is $D\phi/Dt$.

The material derivative converts between the two: it gives the Lagrangian rate using the Eulerian fields we actually measure or compute.

Derivation: it is just the chain rule. A particle’s position is $(x(t),y(t),z(t))$, moving with the flow velocity $\vec u=u\hat i+v\hat j+w\hat k$, so $dx/dt=u$, $dy/dt=v$, $dz/dt=w$. Then

\[\frac{D\phi}{Dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\phi\big(x(t),y(t),z(t),t\big) =\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt},\]

that is,

\[\underbrace{\frac{D\phi}{Dt}}_{\text{total}}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}}_{\text{local}}+\underbrace{u\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}}_{\text{convective}} =\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\phi .\]

Here $\vec u\cdot\nabla=u\,\partial/\partial x+v\,\partial/\partial y+w\,\partial/\partial z$ is a scalar operator. The convective term $(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\phi=\vec u\cdot\nabla\phi$ is the change due to the bulk motion carrying the particle to where $\phi$ is different. It is $\lvert\vec u\rvert$ times the directional derivative of §2 along the flow direction.

Term Nonzero when Example
Local the field changes in time at a fixed point (unsteady) the whole river warms up during the day
Convective the particle moves through a non-uniform field water flows from a shaded pool into a sunlit stretch

Example: steady flow through a nozzle. In a converging pipe (section A wide, section B narrow) with steady flow, $\partial\vec u/\partial t=\vec0$: at every fixed point the velocity never changes. Yet each particle speeds up from A to B, because it moves into a region of higher velocity. That acceleration is purely convective. Steady flow does not mean zero acceleration.

When $\phi$ is a vector: acceleration. Applying $D/Dt$ to each component of the velocity $\vec u=u\hat i+v\hat j+w\hat k$ gives the acceleration of a fluid particle, the $\vec a$ in Newton’s second law for fluids:

\[\vec a=\frac{D\vec u}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\vec u}{\partial t}+(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec u,\] \[\begin{aligned} a_x&=\frac{Du}{Dt}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z},\\ a_y&=\frac{Dv}{Dt}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial v}{\partial z},\\ a_z&=\frac{Dw}{Dt}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}. \end{aligned}\]

The convective part is the Jacobian of §4 acting on the velocity: $(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec u=J\vec u$ with $J_{ij}=\partial u_i/\partial x_j$. It is nonlinear in $\vec u$ (velocity times velocity gradient), which is the root of much of the difficulty of fluid mechanics, including turbulence.

Worked example. A 2-D, unsteady, incompressible flow has streamwise and cross-stream velocity

\[u(x,y,t)=\frac{Ax}{1+Bt},\qquad v(x,y,t)=\frac{-Ay}{1+Bt},\qquad A=2\ \text{s}^{-1},\quad B=0.5\ \text{s}^{-1}.\]

At $t=2$ s, find the local, convective and total acceleration, in general and at $(x,y)=(2,\ 1.5)$ m. Since the flow is 2-D, $w=0$ and $\partial/\partial z=0$, so the $w\,\partial/\partial z$ terms drop out.

Check incompressibility first: $\partial u/\partial x+\partial v/\partial y=\frac{A}{1+Bt}-\frac{A}{1+Bt}=0$ ✓. This is a stagnation-point flow (streamlines $xy=$ const, fluid arriving along $y$ and leaving along $x$) that weakens over time. We first get the fields in $x,y$, then evaluate at the point. At $t=2$ s, $1+Bt=2$, $A^2=4$ s⁻², $AB=1$ s⁻².

1. Local acceleration $\partial\vec u/\partial t$:

\[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\frac{ABx}{(1+Bt)^2}=-\frac{x}{4},\qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=+\frac{ABy}{(1+Bt)^2}=+\frac{y}{4}.\]

Negative in $x$ for $x>0$: at a fixed point the flow is slowing down as time passes.

2. Convective acceleration $(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec u$, using $\partial u/\partial x=A/(1+Bt)$, $\partial u/\partial y=0$, $\partial v/\partial x=0$, $\partial v/\partial y=-A/(1+Bt)$:

\[u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\frac{A^2x}{(1+Bt)^2}=x,\qquad u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=\frac{A^2y}{(1+Bt)^2}=y .\]

Positive: particles speed up as they move away from the stagnation point along $x$, and the $y$-velocity (directed toward the axis) shrinks in magnitude as they approach it.

3. Total acceleration $D\vec u/Dt$ = local + convective:

\[a_x=\frac{A(A-B)\,x}{(1+Bt)^2}=0.75\,x,\qquad a_y=\frac{A(A+B)\,y}{(1+Bt)^2}=1.25\,y\qquad(\text{m/s}^2,\ x,y\ \text{in m}).\]

At the point $(x,y)=(2,\ 1.5)$ m, $t=2$ s:

Lagrangian check. Follow one particle instead: $dx/dt=Ax/(1+Bt)$ integrates to $x(t)=x_0(1+Bt)^{A/B}$. Differentiating twice gives

\[\ddot x=x_0\frac{A}{B}\Big(\frac{A}{B}-1\Big)B^2(1+Bt)^{A/B-2}=\frac{A(A-B)}{(1+Bt)^2}\,x,\]

exactly $a_x$ above (and similarly $a_y$ with exponent $-A/B$). The Eulerian formula $D/Dt$ and riding with the particle agree, as they must.

Recap

Topic Core idea
Derivative $\phi’(x)=dy/dx$: scalar slope of a single-variable function.
Partial derivative Slope along one coordinate, others held fixed.
Gradient $\nabla f=[\partial f/\partial x,\ \partial f/\partial y,\ \partial f/\partial z]^T$: vector of steepest ascent, magnitude = steepest slope.
Directional derivative $D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u=\lvert\nabla f\rvert\cos\theta$, with $\lvert\hat u\rvert=1$.
Extremes Max $+\lvert\nabla f\rvert$ at $\theta=0$; zero along contours; min $-\lvert\nabla f\rvert$ at $\theta=\pi$.
Ski hill at $(10,50)$ $\nabla f=(-1,-1)$, ascent $\hat u=-(\hat i+\hat j)/\sqrt2$, steepest descent slope $-\sqrt2$.
Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial v_i/\partial x_j$: rows are the gradients of each component of $\vec v$.
Taylor (multivariable) $f\approx f_0+\nabla f^T\Delta\vec x+\tfrac12\Delta\vec x^TH\Delta\vec x$, $H_{ij}=\partial^2f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$.
Optimality $\nabla f=\vec0$ is necessary, not sufficient.
Gradient descent $\vec x_{k+1}=\vec x_k-\alpha\nabla f$; any $\vec p$ with $\nabla f\cdot\vec p<0$ is a descent direction.
Hessian test All $\lambda>0$: min; all $\lambda<0$: max; mixed: saddle; a zero: inconclusive.
Newton $H\Delta\vec x=-\nabla f$: uses curvature; fast near the optimum.
2-D test $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$: $D>0,f_{xx}>0$ min; $D>0,f_{xx}<0$ max; $D<0$ saddle; $D=0$ inconclusive.
Why it works $\Delta f\approx\frac{1}{2f_{xx}}[(f_{xx}h+f_{xy}k)^2+Dk^2]$ (completing the square).
Material derivative $D\phi/Dt=\partial\phi/\partial t+(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\phi$: local + convective.
Fluid acceleration $\vec a=\partial\vec u/\partial t+(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec u$; nonzero even in steady flow.

Practice question

Q1. On the same hill, you stand at $(20,20)$. Find the steepest-descent direction, its slope, and the slope if you instead ski due east.

View Answer

$\nabla f(20,20)=(-0.1\cdot20,\ -0.02\cdot20)=(-2,-0.4)$, $\lvert\nabla f\rvert=\sqrt{4+0.16}=2.04$.

Steepest descent: $\hat s=-\nabla f/\lvert\nabla f\rvert=(2,0.4)/2.04=(0.981,0.196)$, mostly east. Slope $=-2.04$.

Due east, $\hat u=(1,0)$: $D_{\hat u}f=-2$, almost as steep, because east is only $11.3^\circ$ from the fall line.
